DRAFT SENTENCES CURRENTLY IN THE TEX FILE (placeholders to rewrite in your own words, one per line)
  S1. The European problem uses the NVIDIA (NVDA) closing price of \$\NvdaS\ on 2026-10-05, a strike
      $K=S_0$ (at the money) and a maturity of one year, which is 252 trading days.
  S2. Time is measured in trading years, so a tree step of one day is $\Delta t=1/252$.
  S3. The volatility is the standard deviation of the last 250 daily log returns multiplied by
      $\sqrt{252}$, which gives $\sigma=\NvdaSigma\%$.
  S4. Using a historical volatility assumes that the next year looks like the last one; an implied
      volatility from market quotes would replace it in practice.
  S5. The risk-free rate is the Bank of Canada one-year Treasury-bill yield, $r=\NvdaR\%$, chosen to
      match the one-year maturity.
  S6. A Canadian rate is not the natural choice for a US-dollar asset, but the call is not very
      sensitive to it: its rho is about \BSrhoPct\ dollars per percentage point of the rate
      (Section~\ref{sec:B-greeks}).
  S7. The American put uses a deliberately simple baseline, $S_0=K=10$, $\sigma=20\%$, $r=2\%$,
      $T=1$ and a real-world drift $\mu=5\%$, for which the perpetual limit is known exactly.
  S8. Because the exercise boundary scaled by the strike depends only on $\sigma$, $r$ and the time
      to maturity, results for this baseline carry over to any strike; Table~\ref{tbl:params} lists
      both parameter sets.
