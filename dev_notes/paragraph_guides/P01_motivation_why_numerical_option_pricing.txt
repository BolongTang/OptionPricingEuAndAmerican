ROLE IN THE ARGUMENT
Opens the report. It must make a reader who has not seen the project understand why discrete and
continuous models are compared and why the cost of a method matters.

CONTEXT THE READER ALREADY HAS / WHAT YOU MUST ESTABLISH
The reader knows what a call and a put are and has met Black-Scholes, but has not thought about how
an exchange or a bank actually produces a price for thousands of contracts several times a day. No

SENTENCE PLAN (write in this order; the numbers in brackets are sentence counts)
  S1-S2  [2 sentences]  FOCUS: The practical problem: a closed-form price exists only for the simplest contracts, so desks rely on trees, simulation and PDE solvers, and each of these costs computing time and carries an error that has to be controlled.
  S3-S4  [2 sentences]  FOCUS: The two modelling languages: discrete time (random walk, binomial tree) and continuous time (Brownian motion, geometric Brownian motion). They must agree in the limit; the interesting question is how fast and at what price.
  S5-S6  [2 sentences]  FOCUS: The two contracts: a European call, which has a closed form and therefore a perfect benchmark, and an American put, which has no closed form because the holder can exercise early and the exercise boundary is part of the unknown.
  S7  [1 sentence]  FOCUS: The question the report answers, stated as a decision: which method should be used for which job, with measured errors and run times.

DRAFT SENTENCES CURRENTLY IN THE TEX FILE (placeholders to rewrite in your own words, one per line)
  S1. Option prices are needed continuously, for thousands of contracts at a time, both to quote
      them and to measure the risk of a book.
  S2. A closed-form price exists only for the simplest contracts, so in practice trees, Monte Carlo
      simulation and PDE solvers do most of the work, and each of them trades accuracy against
      computing time.
  S3. These methods come from two modelling languages: a discrete-time language, in which the stock
      follows a random walk on a lattice, and a continuous-time language, in which it follows a
      geometric Brownian motion.
  S4. The two languages describe the same market in the limit of small time steps, so the useful
      question is not whether they agree but how quickly, and at what computational price.
  S5. We study two contracts that stress the methods in different ways.
  S6. The European call on NVIDIA has a closed-form Black--Scholes price and is therefore a clean
      benchmark, whereas the American put has no closed form because its holder may exercise at any
      time and the optimal exercise boundary has to be found together with the price.
  S7. The question we answer is a practical one: which method should be used for which job, given
      measured errors and measured run times?
