ROLE IN THE ARGUMENT
Shows how large the hedging error of a short call is for realistic rebalancing frequencies, the
first time the report speaks about risk and not only about price.

CONTEXT THE READER ALREADY HAS / WHAT YOU MUST ESTABLISH
The reader knows the replication argument. The experiment: the seller receives the Black-Scholes
price, holds N-dependent delta, rebalances n times and finances at r; 20,000 paths.

SENTENCE PLAN (write in this order; the numbers in brackets are sentence counts)
  S1-S2  [2 sentences]  FOCUS: Set-up: discounted hedging error epsilon = C0 + sum Delta_k (discounted price increments) - discounted payoff; zero in continuous time.
  S3-S4  [2 sentences]  FOCUS: Result: the standard deviation of epsilon decays like n^{-1/2} (slope \HedgeSlope); with daily rebalancing it is \HedgeSDdaily dollars, \HedgeSDdailyPct percent of the premium, with weekly rebalancing \HedgeSDweekly dollars (\HedgeSDweeklyPct percent).
  S5-S6  [2 sentences]  FOCUS: Measure independence: the mean error is close to zero and the standard deviation is the same under the real-world drift as under Q: the hedge works whatever the drift.
  S7-S8  [2 sentences]  FOCUS: Interpretation: the residual is a sum of gamma-weighted squared-return surprises; halving it costs four times as many trades, which transaction costs make expensive.

DRAFT SENTENCES CURRENTLY IN THE TEX FILE (placeholders to rewrite in your own words, one per line)
  S1. The seller of the call receives the Black--Scholes price $C_0$, holds $\Delta_k=\Phi(d_1)$
      shares at $n$ equally spaced dates and finances the position at rate $r$.
  S2. The discounted profit is \begin{equation}\label{eq:hedgeerr}  \varepsilon=C_0+\sum_{k=0}^{n-
      1}\Delta_k\big(e^{-rt_{k+1}}S_{t_{k+1}}-e^{-rt_k}S_{t_k}\big)-e^{-rT}(S_T-K)^+, \end{equation}
      which is zero in continuous time.
  S3. Table~\ref{tbl:hedgefreq} and Figure~\ref{fig:hedge} show that the standard deviation of
      $\varepsilon$ decays like $n^{-1/2}$, with a fitted slope of \HedgeSlope.
  S4. With daily rebalancing it is \$\HedgeSDdaily, which is \HedgeSDdailyPct\% of the premium, and
      with weekly rebalancing it is \$\HedgeSDweekly, or \HedgeSDweeklyPct\%.
  S5. The mean error is close to zero and the dispersion is the same, within simulation noise, when
      the stock is simulated with the real-world drift instead of the risk-neutral one.
  S6. The hedge therefore works whatever the drift, which is the practical content of the statement
      that $\mu$ does not enter the price.
  S7. The residual error is the sum of $n$ gamma-weighted surprises of the squared return, each of
      size proportional to $1/n$, which explains the square-root law.
  S8. Cutting the hedging risk in half therefore needs four times as many trades, and with
      transaction costs this is where hedging becomes a cost-benefit decision.

DERIVATION / LATEX MATERIAL (use as the skeleton of the displayed equations)
One-step error: $\varepsilon_k\approx\tfrac12\Gamma_kS_k^2\big((\Delta W_k)^2-\sigma^2\Delta t\big)$ (second-order Taylor remainder of the hedged portfolio).
Variance: $\operatorname{Var}\sum_k\varepsilon_k\approx\tfrac12\sigma^4\Delta t^2\sum_k\mathbb E[\Gamma_k^2S_k^4]\cdot 1$ (since $\operatorname{Var}((\Delta W)^2)=2\Delta t^2$) $\Rightarrow$ sd $\propto\Delta t^{1/2}=(T/n)^{1/2}$.
