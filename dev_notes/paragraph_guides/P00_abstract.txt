DRAFT SENTENCES CURRENTLY IN THE TEX FILE (placeholders to rewrite in your own words, one per line)
  S1. We compare discrete-time and continuous-time models for pricing and hedging options on two
      problems: a one-year at-the-money European call on NVIDIA and an American put.
  S2. A scaled symmetric random walk converges to Brownian motion, with a Kolmogorov distance at the
      terminal time that falls like $N^{-1/2}$ with the exact constant \KSconst.
  S3. Path functionals such as the running maximum converge at the same slow rate, which is why
      discretely monitored barriers need a correction.
  S4. The CRR tree reaches the Black--Scholes price of \$\BSprice\ to within \$\CRRtenkErr\ at
      $N=10{,}000$ steps, whereas the Euler--Maruyama Monte Carlo price has a standard error of
      \$\MCbestSE\ at $5\cdot10^5$ paths.
  S5. A Leisen--Reimer tree with only 101 steps is accurate to \$\DeskLrErr, and a control variate
      reduces the Monte Carlo variance by a factor of \VRcv.
  S6. For the American put, a CRR tree, a finite-difference solver for the variational inequality
      and least-squares Monte Carlo agree on a value of about \TreeRichV\ for the baseline contract,
      and the early-exercise premium is \EEPatmPct\% of the European put at the money.
  S7. On the NVIDIA inputs the American put is worth \$\NvdaPutAm, \NvdaPutEEPpct\% more than the
      European put.
  S8. Daily delta hedging of the call leaves a hedging error with a standard deviation of
      \HedgeSDdailyPct\% of the premium, and a wrong volatility distorts the American exercise rule
      asymmetrically: exercising too early costs about twenty times as much as exercising too late
      in our worst cases.
