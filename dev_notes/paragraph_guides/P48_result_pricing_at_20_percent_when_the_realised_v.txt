      optimal one when the realised volatility is lower than $20\%$, so the buyer exercises too
      late.
  S2. When the realised volatility is higher the $20\%$ boundary lies above the optimal one, and the
      buyer exercises too early.
  S3. At a realised volatility of $10\%$ the mean stopping time is \MisLowTau\ against
      \MisLowTauOpt\ for the optimal rule, and the buyer loses \MisLowLoss\ relative to optimal
      exercise.
  S4. Waiting too long costs a modest amount because the option is still close to the money.
  S5. At $30\%$ the mean stopping time is \MisHighTau\ against \MisHighTauOpt, and the loss relative
      to optimal exercise is \MisHighLoss.
  S6. Exercising too early therefore costs more than exercising too late by a factor of more than
      two in this example.
  S7. The mean profit itself, \MisLowPnL\ at $10\%$ and \MisHighPnL\ at $30\%$, is driven mostly by
      the price mismatch, since the option is worth less or more than the buyer paid.
  S8. The differences relative to the optimal rule are estimated with a standard error of up to
      \RegretSE\ at $10{,}000$ paths, so the loss at $10\%$ is only about two standard errors, which
